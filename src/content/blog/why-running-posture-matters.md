Adding kilometres is the obvious way to get faster. Running those kilometres efficiently is the cheaper one. Small changes in how you carry yourself can reduce the load per step without any extra training.

## Film yourself first

You cannot fix what you have not seen. Set a phone on the ground about 10 metres away, run past it twice — once from the side, once head-on — and record at the highest frame rate your phone offers. Do it at an easy pace, not a sprint.

## What to look for

### Cadence

Most recreational runners land somewhere between 155 and 170 steps per minute. If you are well below that at an easy pace, your stride is likely long and your foot is landing well ahead of your hips, which sends braking force straight up the leg. Nudging cadence up 5% usually shortens the overstride on its own.

### Torso lean

A small forward lean **from the ankles**, not the waist, keeps your hips underneath you. Bending at the waist drops your hips behind your foot strike and shuts down the glutes.

### Arm carriage

Arms swing front-to-back, elbows near 90 degrees, hands relaxed. Arms crossing the midline usually means the torso is rotating, which wastes energy and stresses the lower back.

### Hip drop

Watch the head-on video. If one hip drops noticeably each time you land on the opposite leg, that is a strength issue rather than a technique issue — single-leg work (step-ups, side planks, single-leg deadlifts) helps more than cueing.

## Change one thing at a time

Pick a single cue for a two-week block, and use it only during easy runs at first. Technique changes need low-stress reps to become automatic; trying to hold a new posture during a hard interval session just means you revert under fatigue.

## Re-film every four to six weeks

Perception lies — video does not. Runward's Posture Analysis scores head, shoulder, torso, and limb positions from a video you upload, so you can compare the same angles over time instead of relying on how a run felt.
