Long distance running rewards patience more than talent. Almost everyone who quits in the first three months quits for the same two reasons: they ran too fast, or they added distance too quickly. This guide shows you how to avoid both.

## Week one: run less than you think you can

Your first goal is not distance — it is consistency. Start with three runs a week, 20 to 30 minutes each, and walk whenever you need to. A run/walk pattern such as 4 minutes running, 1 minute walking is a legitimate training method, not a compromise.

Three easy sessions a week beats five hard ones you cannot repeat.

## How slow is "easy"?

Easy running should feel conversational. If you cannot speak a full sentence out loud, you are running too fast for a base-building run. Two practical checks:

- **Talk test:** you can hold a conversation without gasping.
- **Heart rate:** roughly 65–75% of your maximum heart rate.

Around 80% of your weekly running should sit at this effort. This is the single change that turns beginners into long distance runners.

## Build distance with the 10% rule

Increase your total weekly distance by no more than about 10% per week, and take an easier week every fourth week where you drop back by roughly 20%. A typical eight-week ramp for a new runner looks like this:

| Week | Weekly distance | Longest run |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 12 km | 4 km |
| 2 | 13 km | 5 km |
| 3 | 15 km | 6 km |
| 4 | 12 km (easier) | 5 km |
| 5 | 17 km | 7 km |
| 6 | 19 km | 8 km |
| 7 | 21 km | 10 km |
| 8 | 16 km (easier) | 7 km |

The long run is the session that builds endurance. Add distance to it gradually — 1 to 2 km at a time — and run it slower than your other runs.

## Strength and mobility matter more than stretching

Two 20-minute sessions a week are enough to protect your knees, hips and calves:

- Squats and split squats
- Single-leg calf raises (3 sets of 15 per leg)
- Glute bridges and side-lying leg raises
- Planks and dead bugs for trunk stability

Runners who neglect calf and glute strength are the runners who develop Achilles pain and runner's knee.

## Shoes, surfaces and cadence

Buy shoes that fit your foot rather than the model your friend uses, and replace them around 600–800 km. Mix surfaces where you can — a portion of your easy running on softer ground reduces repetitive load. If your steps feel heavy or you land with a straight leg well ahead of your body, gently lifting your cadence to around 165–180 steps per minute usually shortens your stride and softens each landing.

## Signals to respect

Muscle soreness that settles within 48 hours is normal. Pain that is sharp, one-sided, worsens during a run, or makes you limp is not. Take two easy days, and if it persists after a week of reduced load, get it assessed.

Sleep and food are training inputs, not extras. Under-fuelled long runs are the fastest route to stalled progress.

## A realistic first 12 weeks

1. **Weeks 1–4:** establish three to four runs a week, all easy, longest run up to 6 km.
2. **Weeks 5–8:** extend the long run to 10 km, keep everything conversational, add strength work.
3. **Weeks 9–12:** hold the volume, add one light faster session per week — for example 6 × 1 minute quicker with easy jogging between.

After that, you have a base that can carry you to a 10K, a half marathon, or trail running.

## Track it so you can see the pattern

Progress at long distances is invisible week to week and obvious across months. Log every run — distance, effort, how you felt — so that trends, not single sessions, guide your decisions.
